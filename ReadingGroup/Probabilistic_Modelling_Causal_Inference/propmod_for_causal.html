Paper Overview

Position Paper presented at ICML 2025 (42nd International Conference on Machine Learning)

  • Authors: Bruno Mlodozeniec, David Krueger, Richard E. Turner
  • arXiv: 2512.23408
  • Main Claim: You can answer any causal inference question within the realm of probabilistic modelling and inference, without causal-specific tools or notation

“Always write down the probability of everything.” — Steve Gull (MacKay, 2003)

Motivation: Why This Paper?

Confusion in the ML community about tools needed for causal inference:

  • Prevalent claims that you need bespoke causal frameworks (do-notation, SCMs)
  • Many papers state: “causal questions cannot be answered with statistical tools alone”

Examples from the literature:

“None of those questions can be answered with statistical tools alone, but require methods from causality…” — Pawlowski et al. (2020)

“We need to enrich our language with a do-operator” — Pearl (2019)

The Pearl-Gelman Disagreement

Judea Pearl’s View:

“There is no way to answer causal questions without snapping out of statistical vocabulary”

  • Emphasizes the Ladder of Causation
  • Advocates for do-calculus
  • Sees causal inference as distinct from statistics

Andrew Gelman’s View:

“I find it baffling that Pearl and his colleagues keep taking statistical problems and, to my mind, complicating them by wrapping them in a causal structure”

  • Views causal tools as potentially unnecessary complexity
  • Prefers traditional statistical/Bayesian approaches

The Paper’s Position: The disagreement is primarily semantic — revolving around the meaning of “statistical”

What the Paper Claims (and Doesn’t)

Claims:

  • Causal toolbox is not necessary for causal inference
  • Standard probabilistic modelling is sufficient

Does NOT Claim:

  • Causal tools are without utility
  • Pearl’s framework should be abandoned
  • A new framework is being proposed

The goal is to clarify how causal problems can be tackled within probabilistic modelling

The Core Principle

Always write down the joint probability over all the settings you are interested in.

This means:

  1. Observed setting: The world as we observe it
  2. Interventional setting: What happens if we do something
  3. Counterfactual setting: What would have happened if…

All can be modelled by specifying a joint distribution over all relevant variables and settings!

Interventions

Example: Aspirin and Headaches

Setting: Study aspirin’s effect on headache duration

Variables:

  • \(Z\) — Initial headache severity
  • \(T\) — Aspirin dosage
  • \(Y\) — Headache duration

Model specification (log-normal): \[ \begin{aligned} Z &\sim \log\mathcal{N}(\mu_Z, \sigma_Z^2) \\ T &= Z^a \varepsilon_T \quad \text{where } \varepsilon_T \sim \log\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_T^2) \\ Y &= Z^b T^c \varepsilon_Y \quad \text{where } \varepsilon_Y \sim \log\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_Y^2) \end{aligned} \]

The Causal Question

Interventional Question: What is the expected effect of intervening to assign someone a given dose of aspirin \(t^*\) on their headache duration?

The Naïve (Wrong) Approach:

Simply compute \(\mathbb{E}[Y | T = t^*]\) from observational data

Problem: Simpson’s Paradox!

Even when \(c > 0\) (aspirin helps), we might observe that people who take more aspirin have longer headaches on average — because they had more severe headaches to begin with!

Simpson’s Paradox Illustrated

Conditional expectation (when \(c = 0\), aspirin has no effect):

\[ \mathbb{E}[Y | T] = \exp\left(\frac{ab\sigma_Z^2}{\sigma_Z^2 + \sigma_Y^2}(T - a\mu_z) + \text{const.}\right) \]

  • When \(a, b > 0\): Expected headache duration increases with aspirin dose
  • Even though aspirin has no effect on headache duration!

The longer headache in those who took more aspirin is due to confounding (more severe initial headache), not adverse effects of aspirin.

Key Assumptions:

  1. Parameters \(\boldsymbol{\Theta}\) are shared between worlds
  2. \(T^*\) is independent of \(Z^*\) (we intervene!)
  3. Mechanism \(p_\theta(y|t,z)\) stays the same

\[q_\theta(t) = \delta(t^* - t)\] \[q_\theta(y|t,z) = p_\theta(y|t,z)\]

The Joint Distribution

The complete joint over both settings:

\[ \begin{aligned} p(z^*, t^*, y^*, \boldsymbol{\theta}, \mathcal{D}) = &\underbrace{p_\theta(z^*) q(t^*) p_\theta(y^*|z^*, t^*)}_{\text{Interventional world}} \\ &\times \underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{N} p_\theta(z_i) p_\theta(t_i|z_i) p_\theta(y_i|z_i, t_i)}_{\text{Observed world}} \times p(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \end{aligned} \]

Computing the causal effect:

\[ \mathbb{E}[Y^* | \mathcal{D}] = \int \underbrace{\iint y^* q_\theta(y^*|z^*, t^*) q_\theta(z^*) \, dz^* \, dy^*}_{\text{Expectation in intervened world}} \underbrace{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D}) \, d\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{Posterior from data}} \]

Result: True Causal Effect

For the log-normal aspirin model:

\[ \mathbb{E}[Y^* | \boldsymbol{\theta}] = (t^*)^{-c} \exp\left(b\mu_Z + \frac{b^2\sigma_Z^2 + \sigma_Y^2}{2}\right) \]

Key Insight:

  • Effect only depends on parameter \(c\)
  • When \(c > 0\): Higher dose \(\Rightarrow\) shorter headache
  • No confounding — we correctly identified the causal effect!

Classical Approach: do-Notation

The intervention operation on a Bayesian Network:

Definition (Intervention in BNs): An intervention on node \(j\) with new parents \(\mathbf{PA}_j^*\) and new conditional \(g^*(x_j|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{PA}_j^*})\) yields:

\[ q(\mathbf{x}) = g^*(x_j|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{PA}_j^*}) \prod_{i \in \{1,\ldots,d\} \setminus \{j\}} p(x_i|\mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{PA}_i^{\mathcal{G}}}) \]

do-notation shorthand:

\[p(\mathbf{x} | do(X_k = c)) = p_{do(X_k := \delta(\cdot - c))}(\mathbf{x})\]

Confounding: effect of \(T\) on \(Y\) is confounded when \(p(y|t) \neq p(y|do(T=t))\)

Relationship: Probabilistic vs. Causal Framework

Aspect Twin Model (Probabilistic) Causal BN + do-calculus
Notation Standard probability Specialized (\(do\), \(\perp\!\!\!\perp\))
Transparency Explicit joint model Graph + rules
Flexibility Any model structure Graphical models only
Learning curve Lower Higher

Both approaches are mathematically equivalent — the causal framework provides syntactic sugar for specifying the same joint!

Counterfactuals

Counterfactual Question

Counterfactual Inference: What would have happened to a specific individual if they had received a different treatment?

Example:

A friend took dose \(t\) of aspirin and her headache lasted \(y\) hours. Had she taken a larger dose \(t^*\), would her headache have gone away sooner?

Key Difference from Interventions:

  • Interventions: population-level effects
  • Counterfactuals: individual-level, sharing variables between worlds

Counterfactual Inference

Target: \(p(y^* | t, y, \mathcal{D})\)

\[ p(y^* | t, y, \mathcal{D}) = \mathbb{E}_{p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathcal{D}, t, y)} \left[ \int p_\theta(y^* | t^*, z) p_\theta(z | t, y) \, dz \right] \]

Interpretation:

  1. Infer the latent headache severity \(z\) from observed \((t, y)\)
  2. Predict counterfactual outcome \(y^*\) given \(z\) and new treatment \(t^*\)
  3. Average over parameter uncertainty

Which Latent Variables to Share?

Important Consideration: Should we share all sources of randomness?

Example: The noise \(\varepsilon_Y\) in \(Y = Z^b T^c \varepsilon_Y\)

  • Could represent person-specific health attributes (weight, age, BMI) → Share!
  • Could represent environmental factors or manufacturing tolerances → Don’t share!

Whether to share noise variables depends on what they represent and the specific counterfactual question!

Structural Causal Models (SCMs)

Definition (SCM): A structural causal model \(\mathfrak{C}\) on variables \(\mathbf{X}\) consists of:

  1. A directed acyclic graph \(\mathcal{G}\) on \(\mathbf{X}\)
  2. Structural assignments: \(X_j := f_j(\mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{PA}_j}, N_j)\)
  3. Distribution \(p_\mathbf{N}\) over independent noise variables \(\{N_1, \ldots, N_d\}\)

Counterfactuals in SCMs:

Given observation \(\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}\), the counterfactual SCM uses:

\[p_{\mathbf{N}^*}(\mathbf{n}) = p_\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{n} | \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x})\]

(Posterior over noise becomes prior for counterfactual)

SCMs vs. Probabilistic Approach

SCMs require sharing ALL noise variables:

“Formally, SCMs contain strictly more information than their corresponding graph and law” — Peters et al. (2017)

But this can be overly restrictive:

  • What if some noise represents modelling error, not real stochasticity?
  • What if we only want to share some sources of randomness?

SCMs are sufficient but not necessary for counterfactual inference. The probabilistic framework is more flexible.

Benefits of the Probabilistic Framework

Why Probabilistic Modelling?

1. Flexibility:

  • Handle settings beyond graphical models (e.g., probabilistic programming)
  • “Causal graphs cannot easily express many real-world phenomena” — Park et al. (2023)

2. Natural handling of multiple data sources:

  • Combine interventional and observational data naturally

3. No prescribed inference order:

  • SCMs require “abduction, action, prediction” steps
  • Probabilistic approach: condition on whatever you like!

Lowering the Barrier to Entry

The probabilistic framework:

  • Accessible to ML community already familiar with probabilistic modelling
  • No need to learn specialized causal notation (\(do\)-calculus, etc.)
  • Assumptions are explicit in the joint model

“Understanding that causal tools can be seen as syntactic shorthands for specifying joint distributions is, in our view, productive towards [research] endeavours.”

The “Ladder of Causation”

Pearl’s three-level hierarchy:

Level Type Question
1 Association \(P(Y|X)\) — What if I see?
2 Intervention \(P(Y|do(X))\) — What if I do?
3 Counterfactual \(P(Y_{x'}|X=x, Y=y)\) — What if I had done?

The Paper’s View: All three levels can be addressed within probabilistic modelling by writing down the appropriate joint distribution!

Resolving the Debate

The Core Issue: The term “statistical” is often used too narrowly

  • Pearl uses “statistical” to mean only “associational” or “observational”
  • This restricted definition is the basis for claims that statistics is insufficient

But statistics has always dealt with:

  • Non-static settings (domain shift, off-policy RL)
  • Subjective assumptions
  • Inference beyond the observed distribution

Conclusion

Key Takeaways

  1. Causal inference ⊆ Probabilistic modelling
    • No need for bespoke causal frameworks
  2. The recipe:
    • Write down the joint over ALL settings of interest
    • Use standard probability theory for inference
  3. Causal tools are syntactic sugar:
    • do-notation, SCMs, do-calculus are convenient but not necessary
    • They emerge from probabilistic modelling
  4. The debate is semantic:
    • Disagreement stems from narrow definitions of “statistics”

The “One Key Takeaway”

Always write down the joint probability over all the settings you are interested in.

This simple principle:

  • Makes assumptions explicit
  • Enables flexible causal reasoning
  • Unifies interventional and counterfactual inference
  • Lowers barriers for the ML community

Discussion Points

  1. Practical Implications:
    • Should we teach causal inference differently?
    • When are causal-specific tools still valuable?
  2. Limitations:
    • Identifiability concerns still apply
    • Model specification is still challenging
    • Hidden confounders remain problematic
  3. Future Directions:
    • Integration with probabilistic programming
    • Deep learning and causal inference
    • Causal representation learning

Appendix

Appendix: Log-normal Distribution

Definition (Log-normal): A random variable \(X\) is log-normal with parameters \((\mu, \sigma^2)\) if:

\[X \sim \log\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \iff \log X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\]

Properties:

  • Product of log-normals is log-normal: \(X_1 X_2 \sim \log\mathcal{N}(\mu_1 + \mu_2, \sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2)\)
  • Power of log-normal is log-normal: \(X^c \sim \log\mathcal{N}(c\mu, c^2\sigma^2)\)
  • Expected value: \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \exp(\mu + \sigma^2/2)\)

Appendix: Bayesian Networks

Definition (BN): Random variables \(\mathbf{X} = (X_1, \ldots, X_d)\) form a Bayesian Network with respect to DAG \(\mathcal{G}\) if:

\[X_i \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{ND}_i^{\mathcal{G}}} | \mathbf{X}_{\mathbf{PA}_i^{\mathcal{G}}}\]

where \(\mathbf{ND}_i\) are non-descendants and \(\mathbf{PA}_i\) are parents.

Factorization:

\[p(x_1, \ldots, x_d) = \prod_{i=1}^{d} p(x_i | \mathbf{x}_{\mathbf{PA}_i^{\mathcal{G}}})\]

Appendix: do-Calculus Rules

For a Causal Graphical Model with graph \(\mathcal{G}\) and disjoint subsets \(\mathbf{X}, \mathbf{Y}, \mathbf{Z}, \mathbf{W}\):

  1. Insertion/deletion of observations: \[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{w}, do(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x})) = p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w}, do(\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}))\] if \(\mathbf{Y} \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf{Z} | \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{W}\) in \(\mathcal{G}_{\bar{\mathbf{X}}}\)

  2. Action/observation exchange: \[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w}, do(\mathbf{X}:=\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z})) = p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{z}, \mathbf{w}, do(\mathbf{X}:=\mathbf{x}))\] if \(\mathbf{Y} \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf{Z} | \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{W}\) in \(\mathcal{G}_{\bar{\mathbf{X}}\underline{\mathbf{Z}}}\)

  3. Insertion/deletion of actions: \[p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w}, do(\mathbf{X}:=\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{z})) = p(\mathbf{y}|\mathbf{w}, do(\mathbf{X}:=\mathbf{x}))\] if \(\mathbf{Y} \perp\!\!\!\perp \mathbf{Z} | \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{W}\) in \(\mathcal{G}_{\bar{\mathbf{X}}\underline{\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{W})}}\)

Appendix: Identifiability

Definition: An interventional conditional probability (the “causal effect”) is identifiable if it can be expressed in terms of probability functions of the observed variables only.

Bayesian Perspective:

  • Identifiability is not a requirement for inference
  • Even with non-identifiability, Bayesian learning can still occur
  • The posterior can differ from the prior, reflecting updated beliefs

But: Epistemic uncertainty cannot be fully eliminated without identifiability (even with infinite data)
