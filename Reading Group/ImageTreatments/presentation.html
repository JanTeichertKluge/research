Introduction & Motivation

  • Causal Effect Estimation
    Estimating individual treatment effects (ITEs) in observational studies is challenging due to confounding biases and the lack of ground truth outcomes (Rubin 2005).

  • Why Image Treatments?
    Many studies use scalar or categorical representations for treatments. However, real-world applications (e.g., personalized thumbnails in OTT services or product images in e-commerce) involve rich, multi-dimensional data like images (Harada & Kashima 2021; Kaddour et al. 2021).

  • Objective of the Paper
    Introduce NICE (Network for Image treatments Causal effect Estimation) to harness image attributes for improved causal effect estimation.

Problem Formulation

  • Notation:
    • \(n \in \mathbb{N}\) users / instances with index \(i\).
    • \(k \in \mathbb{N}\) (discrete) treatments with index \(t\).
      • Treatments are represented by images \(I_t \in \mathcal{I} \subset \mathbb{R}^m\).
      • The assigned treatment for user \(i\) is \(t_i \in \{1,2, ..., k\}\).
    • User covariates \(x_i \in \mathcal{X} \subset \mathbb{R}^d\),
    • (Potential) Outcomes \(y_{i}^{t}\) for user \(i\) with treatment \(t\).

\(\Rightarrow\) The dataset consists of \(n\) pairs: \(\{x_i, I_{t_i}, y_{i}^{t_i}\}_{i=1}^n\).

(They are using the classic assumptions: Unconfoundedness and Overlap/Positivity)

Problem Formulation

  • Theoretical Motivation:
    • Following Rubin-Neyman Potential Outcomes Framework
    • Consider a pair of treatments \(a\) and \(b\).
    • The Individual Treatment Effect (ITE) for user \(i\) is: \[ \tau^{a,b}(x_i) = \mathbb{E}[y^{a}_i | x=x_i] - \mathbb{E}[y^{b}_i | x=x_i] \]
  • Objective:
    • Measurement of the model performance on the Precision in Estimation of Heterogeneous Effect (PEHE) \[ \epsilon_{\text{PEHE}} = \frac{1}{{k \choose 2}} \sum\limits_{a=1}^{k} \sum\limits_{b=0}^{a-1}\left[ \frac{1}{n} \sum\limits_{i=1}^n (\hat{\tau}^{a, b}(\mathbf{x}_i) - \tau^{a, b}(\mathbf{x}_i))^2 \right] \]

Proposed Model: NICE

  • User Covariate Embedding \(\Phi : \mathcal{X} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d_1}\).
  • Treatment Embedding \(\Psi : \Lambda(\mathcal{I}) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^{d_2}\).
  • Treatment Head Networks (fully-connected NNs) \(\pi_t\) for \(t \in \{1, 2, ..., k\}\).
  • Loss Function: \(\mathcal{L} = \alpha \cdot \mathcal{L}_1 + \beta \cdot \mathcal{L}_2\).
  • Where \(\mathcal{L}_1 = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}^{t}_i - y_i)^2\) is MSE-like and
  • \(\mathcal{L}_2 = \binom{k}{2}^{-1} \sum_{a=1}^{k} \sum_{b=1}^{a-1} \mathrm{MMD}\left( \\(\lbrace \Phi; \Psi(\Lambda))\}_{t=a}, \lbrace \Phi; \Psi(\Lambda) \}_{t=b} \right)\) uses Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) to reduce treatment assignment bias.

Proposed Model: NICE

  • NICE Framework Overview
    A neural network architecture that integrates:
    • Representation Learning:
      Learn low-dimensional embeddings for both user covariates and image treatments.
      • Pre-trained models (e.g., ResNet (He et al. 2016) or VGG (Simonyan & Zisserman 2014)) are used to extract image features.
    • Treatment Head Networks:
      Separate networks for each treatment to generate counterfactual predictions.
    • Loss Functions:
      Combination of Mean Square Error (MSE) for outcome prediction and a Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) loss to reduce treatment assignment bias (Sriperumbudur et al. 2012).
      • where \(\mathcal{L}_1\) is the regression loss and \(\mathcal{L}_2\) is the treatment regularization loss.

Data Simulation & Experimental Setup

  • Semi-Synthetic Data Generation
    • Uses real-world poster images from the PosterLens dataset (Aptlin 2021) as treatments.
    • Simulated user covariates and potential outcomes are generated to mimic realistic scenarios.
  • Experimental Design
    • Treatment Groups:
      Experiments with 4, 8, and 16 treatment groups.
    • Baselines Compared:
      TarNet (Shalit et al. 2017), GraphITE (Harada & Kashima 2021), SIN (Kaddour et al. 2021), and CaML (Nilforoshan et al. 2023)
  • Evaluation Metrics
    • PEHE Metric:
      Measures the precision in estimating heterogeneous effects.
    • Zero-Shot Setting:
      Assesses model performance on unseen treatments during training.

Experimental Results & Discussion

  • Quantitative Results
    • NICE consistently outperforms all baseline methods on the PEHE metric.
    • The performance gap increases with the number of treatments and in zero-shot settings.
  • Robustness
    • NICE maintains superior performance under varying treatment assignment biases, highlighting its robustness in real-world scenarios.
  • Takeaway
    • Incorporating the rich, high-dimensional information present in image treatments leads to significantly improved causal effect estimates.

Conclusions & Future Directions

  • Key Contributions
    • Introduces NICE, a framework that leverages image treatments for causal effect estimation.
    • Demonstrates improved ITE estimation compared to existing methods through extensive experiments.
  • Future Research
    • Extending the framework to more complex data types (e.g., video, multimodal data).
    • Enhancing scalability and interpretability of the model.
    • Validating the approach on real-world datasets beyond semi-synthetic simulations.
