  • Utilizing unstructured data enhances decision-making
  • Sources like images and text contain valuable insights

Understanding price elasticity is essential for detailed economic analysis and decision making. Its influence extends to shaping strategies, determining optimal prices, and impacting market dynamics.

Traditional causal methods, usually based on structured / tabular data, have limitations that prevent them from adapting to more complex scenarios.

The use of unstructured data sources represents a significant step forward in improving decision making. These sources, including images and text, provide valuable insights, such as hidden information about confounding variables. As a result, the accuracy of our analysis and predictions can be improved.

Motivating Example - PLR

Partially linear regression model (PLR)

\[ \begin{align*} &\ln(Q) = \ln(P) \theta_0 + g_0(X) + \varepsilon, & &\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon| P,X] = 0, \\ &\ln(P) = m_0(X) + V, & &\mathbb{E}[V | X] = 0, \end{align*} \]

with

  • Demand \(Q\)
  • Price \(P\)
  • Additional product features \(X = (X_1, \dots)\)
  • Stochastic errors \(\varepsilon\) and \(V\)

\(\Rightarrow\) The causal parameter \(\theta_0\) can be interpreted as price elasticity of demand!

The question now is how to estimate this price elasticity!

We define this partial linear regression model with the natural logarithm of the Demand \(Q\) as the outcome and the natural logarithm of the Price \(P\) as the treatment variable. Possible product features are selected as confounders \(X\).

This causal model is shown here as a DAG (directed acyclic graph).

In this context, the causal parameter theta can be considered as price elasticity.

Motivating Example - DML

Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML)

  • Use machine learning methods to fit \[ \begin{align*} l_0(X)&:=\mathbb{E}[\ln(Q)|X]\\ m_0(X)&:=\mathbb{E}[\ln(P)|X] \end{align*} \]
  • Construct the orthogonalized score (either cross-fitting or split the sample) for a fitted nuisance learner \(\hat{\eta}_0:=(\hat{l}_0, \hat{m}_0)\) \[ \psi(W,\theta,\hat{\eta}_0) := \Big(\ln(Q) - \hat{l}_0(X) - \theta\big(\ln(P)-\hat{m}_0(X)\big)\Big)\Big(\ln(P)-\hat{m}_0(X)\Big) \]
  • Compute the estimate as the solution of \[ 0 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \psi(W,\hat{\theta}_0,\hat{\eta}_0) \]
  • We start by using machine learning to fit functions that capture the conditional expectations of the Output / Demand and the treatment / Price Variable given our (unstructured and high dimensional) set of covariates:

\[\begin{align*} l_0(X) &:= \mathbb{E}[\ln(Q)|X] \\ m_0(X) &:= \mathbb{E}[\ln(P)|X] \end{align*}\]

  • Next, we construct an orthogonalized score for a fitted nuisance learner denoted as \(\hat{\eta}_0\), which comprises \(\hat{l}_0\) and \(\hat{m}_0\).

\[ \psi(W, \theta, \hat{\eta}_0) := \left(\ln(Q) - \hat{l}_0(X) - \theta\left(\ln(P) - \hat{m}_0(X)\right)\right)\left(\ln(P) - \hat{m}_0(X)\right) \]

  • On the final step, we calculate the estimate by solving the following equation:

\[ 0 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \psi(W, \hat{\theta}_0, \hat{\eta}_0) \]

Motivating Example - DML


  • Assumptions for the nuisance elements (see Chernozhukov et al. (2018)) \(\lVert \hat{m}_0 - m_0 \rVert_{P,2} \times \big( \lVert \hat{m}_0 - m_0 \rVert_{P,2} + \lVert \hat{\ell}_0 - \ell_0\rVert _{P,2}\big) \le \delta_N N^{-1/2}\)

  • Under some regularity conditions the estimator \(\hat{\theta}_0\) concentrates in \(1/\sqrt{n}\)-neighborhood of \(\theta_0\) and \[ \sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_0 - \theta_0) \to \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2) \]

\(\Rightarrow\) simple approach to control for product features, if they can be used for ML models!

  • We rely on the approach of Chernozhukov et al. (2018)

Nuisance Element Assumptions: We assume that the difference between our estimated nuisance functions (\(\hat{m}_0\) and \(\hat{l}_0\)) and the true functions (\(m_0\) and \(\ell_0\)) is bounded by a factor scaling with the sample size, as given by the equation:

$$
\lVert \hat{m}_0 - m_0 \rVert_{P,2} \times \left(\lVert \hat{m}_0 - m_0 \rVert_{P,2} +  \lVert \hat{\ell}_0 - \ell_0\rVert _{P,2}\right) \le \delta_N N^{-1/2}
$$

Under these assumption and some regularity conditions, our estimator \(\hat{\theta}_0\) converges to a neighborhood of the true parameter \(\theta_0\) at a rate of \(1/\sqrt{n}\). Furthermore, the square root of the sample size times the difference between our estimated parameter and the true parameter converges to a normal distribution:

$$
\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}_0 - \theta_0) \to \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)
$$

  • Utilization of Unstructured Data: DoubleML Deep leverages unstructured data sources, like product descriptions and images, which often contain rich information for enhanced accuracy in estimations.

  • Integration of Structured and Unstructured Data: By combining structured tabular data with unstructured data, the model gains a holistic product view, enabling more robust and precise predictions.

  • Separate Estimations: Separate estimations of ln Q and ln P provide nuanced insights into how various factors impact demand and price, yielding actionable insights.

  • Causal Inference: Well-suited for causal inference, this model combines the learning of the nuisance functions g and m needed for the partial linear regression approach.

  • Advanced Machine Learning Techniques: DoubleML Deep employs advanced machine learning methods to optimize the estimation process, ensuring high-quality results.

  • Model Flexibility: One notable flexibility of the DoubleML Deep model is its adaptability to various models available from the Hugging Face model hub for transformers and models from pytorch wide-deep for the tabular component. Whether it’s the transformer model for processing unstructured data or the tabular model for structured data, the Hugging Face model hub and pytorch wide-deep models offer a diverse range of pre-trained models to choose from.

Now I would like to briefly explain the workflow of DoubleMLDeep in Python.

First of all, some necessary input parameters must be defined. Then the actual DoubleMLDeep network can be created, which uses Hugging Face Transformer models and the Pytorch framework, among other things.

A DoubleMLDeep Data Module must be created and handles the various input data such as texts and images. This uses the Pytorch Lightning Framework.

The DoubleMLDeep Lightning Module also uses Pytorch Lightning and contains additional training parameters.

The network can now be trained with the pytorch Lightning Trainer. The corresponding predictions in the DoubleML package can then be used to obtain the estimate of the causal parameter.

DoubleML Deep - More Details


  • Use pretrained models for unstructured data e.g. Bert and Beit

  • Basic models for tabular data seem sufficient

  • Monitor the nuisance losses

    • \(\lVert \hat{l}_0 - \ln(Q) \rVert_{P,2} \le \lVert \hat{l}_0 -l_0 \rVert_{P,2} + \sigma_{\tilde{\epsilon}}\)
    • \(\lVert \hat{m}_0 - \ln(P) \rVert_{P,2} \le \lVert \hat{m}_0 - m_0 \rVert_{P,2} + \sigma_V\)

  • Unstructured Data Models: We employ pre-trained models like BERT and BEiT to extract valuable information from unstructured data, including product descriptions and images.

  • Tabular Data Models: Basic models for tabular data have proven to be effective.

  • Monitoring Nuisance Losses: To ensure the reliability of our estimations, we diligently monitor nuisance losses. The following conditions are crucial for assessing the quality of our models:

    • \(\lVert \hat{l}_0 - \ln(Q) \rVert_{P,2} \le \lVert \hat{l_0} - l_0 \rVert_{P,2} + \sigma_{\tilde{\epsilon}}\)

    • \(\lVert \hat{m}_0 - \ln(P) \rVert_{P,2} \le \lVert \hat{m_0} - m_0 \rvert_{P,2} + \sigma_V\)

    These conditions help maintain the validity of our estimates by ensuring that the discrepancies between our estimated nuisance functions and their true counterparts, along with stochastic errors, remain within acceptable bounds.

  • Key Variables:

    • Sales Rank: Represents the sales rank, calculated as a weighted mean over the last 30 days. It provides insights into a product’s performance in terms of sales and popularity.

    • Image: Captures the first image associated with the product. Images are crucial for understanding the visual aspects of the product, which can influence customer choices.

    • \(\ln(Q) = \theta_0 \ln(P) + \beta^T X + \epsilon\)
      • \(R^2 = 0.330\)
2.5 % \(\theta\) 97.5 %

  • In our initial baseline estimate, we begin with a simple OLS (Ordinary Least Squares) regression model that does not include covariates ($X). This baseline model serves as a reference point for comparison.

  • The baseline model is formulated as follows:

    \[ \ln(Q) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln(P) + \epsilon \]

    Where:

    • \(\ln(Q)\) represents the natural logarithm of demand.
    • \(\ln(P)\) represents the natural logarithm of price.
    • \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\) are regression coefficients.
    • \(\epsilon\) denotes the error term.

  • The \(R^2\) value for this baseline model, indicating the proportion of variability in demand explained by price, is exceptionally low at \(0.000\). This implies that the model, without considering covariates, has minimal explanatory power.

  • The plot on the right illustrates the centered logarithmic negative sales rank over the centered logarithmic price as a binned plot, helping visualize the relationship between sales rank and price within this baseline model. It becomes evident that the relationship is far from straightforward and requires more advanced modeling techniques to capture the data’s complexity.

  • The 95% confidence interval for \(\beta_1\) is presented below:

    • 2.5%: -0.011
    • Median: 0.0203
    • 97.5%: 0.052

    This baseline serves as the starting point for understanding the relationship between price and demand.
